01 / The problem

Which slopes are creeping, and which are accelerating?

Retreating glaciers and thawing permafrost are destabilising rock slopes along Greenland’s fjords. When one of them fails into the water, the displaced wave can reach communities along the coast.

Some of these slopes creep slowly and indefinitely. Others accelerate along a path to failure. Telling the two apart before failure is what decides which sites need attention, and it remains unresolved. No single technique measures the full range of displacement rates continuously and across a whole slope face. Depending on the site, reported rates span 0.05 to more than 5 metres per year.

Satellite InSAR revisits too slowly to catch short-term acceleration and resolves only the line-of-sight component. Ground GNSS is continuous and precise, but measures single points. UAV photogrammetry maps the entire face, yet only within a short summer window. Active ground radar and LiDAR draw more power than a year-round Arctic site can supply.

Qaarusuup Qaqqaa is included in Greenland’s inventory of unstable slopes and registered mass movements.

02 / Fieldwork in Greenland

Making the measurement possible.

The 2026 campaign ran from 10 July to 9 August, across several Greenland slopes reported as unstable. This season was a prototype trial: build the instrument, get it recording in the field, and find out whether the measurement works at all. Everything shown below comes from Qaarusuup, where the prototype was deployed from 1 to 4 August.

Once installed, the instrument stayed on site and recorded on a schedule with nobody beside it. Timing, power, storage, and signal quality decide whether there is any data to process. In this project they are as much a part of the research as the algorithms.

03 / My research

From detecting a slope to measuring how it moves.

I develop ground-based GNSS passive radar instruments and processing methods to observe unstable rock slopes in Greenland. My long-term goal is to measure how these slopes deform.

The system uses signals from navigation satellites, so it needs no dedicated transmitter. My work connects reliable field recordings with radar observations that can support future slope monitoring.

The sky-facing antenna receives the direct GNSS signal, while the slope-facing antenna receives its reflection. The software-defined radio converts these analogue electrical signals into synchronized digital IQ samples, which the single-board computer currently stores for later processing.

A solar panel, charge controller, battery, and low-voltage disconnector form the power system for unattended operation. The controller manages solar charging, the battery stores energy, and the disconnector cuts the load when battery voltage is low. The diagram shows the power supply to the single-board computer.

04 / Research outputs

Two ways to examine the slope.

I processed the Qaarusuup field recordings into range-Doppler maps and SAR images. Each provides a different view of the reflected GNSS signal, and each leads to a further output.

Range-Doppler observations

I generated range-Doppler maps to examine reflections from the slope. The return from the slope, marked by the white box, stands out clearly above the noise at about 1.3 km of bistatic range, the extra path travelled by the reflected signal.

This shows that a receiver using only navigation-satellite signals can pick out the slope across the fjord. I also used these range-Doppler maps to estimate how strongly each part of the slope scatters.

NBRCS inversion: how strongly the slope scatters

Combining the range-Doppler maps with ArcticDEM, I estimate the normalized bistatic radar cross section (NBRCS) of each part of the slope, which describes how strongly that patch of rock scatters the satellite signal. The maps below compare this estimate with the same quantity derived from the SAR image. Because scattering strength depends on the roughness and material of the rock surface, such maps could help characterise the surface and follow how it changes.

SAR imagery

I used a back-projection algorithm with ArcticDEM elevation to form a SAR image from a 600-second recording. The image places the reflections on the slope itself and shows which parts of the slope return the signal.

Linking each reflection to a location on the slope makes it possible to ask where a change happens. Repeat acquisitions could support surface-change analysis and interferometry, shown below.

Interferometry: comparing repeat SAR images

I pair two SAR images taken from the same satellite geometry at different times to form an interferogram. Its phase shifts with small movements of the surface, and coherence shows where that phase can be trusted. Over much of the slope the coherence stays high between the two images, so the phase there could be used to monitor slope movement. A ground receiver can repeat this far more often than satellite radar revisits, and it maps the slope face rather than single points. Unlike active ground-based radar, it is built from low-cost parts and needs no transmitter of its own. It could therefore complement existing tools for monitoring slope movement.

05 / My role

Engineering the connection between fieldwork and measurement.

I develop and integrate the radar instrument, deploy it in the field, and develop the processing chain from raw IQ data to radar imagery and the outputs built on it. This is the instrument-to-image work presented here within the broader NNA Greenland Hazards Project.

06 / What next

Toward autonomous slope monitoring.

For the planned 2027 deployment, I am working toward a system that can remain in the field for a year, process observations on site, and send results back to my home institution.

Reliable field operation

Improve power, weather protection, and timing stability for unattended operation through the Arctic seasons.

Onboard processing and transfer

Run the processing chain on the single-board computer and transmit processed outputs, ultimately including interferograms and validated displacement estimates.

Repeat measurements

Extend interferometry from a few days of acquisitions to a full year, and develop the analysis needed to turn repeated interferograms into measurements of slope deformation.
