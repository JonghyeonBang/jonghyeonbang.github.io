01 / The problem

Which slopes are creeping, and which are accelerating?

Retreating glaciers and thawing permafrost are destabilising rock slopes along Greenland’s fjords. When one of them fails into the water, the displaced wave can reach communities along the coast.

Some of these slopes creep slowly and indefinitely. Others accelerate along a path to failure. Telling the two apart before failure is what decides which sites need attention, and it remains unresolved. No single technique measures the full range of displacement rates continuously and across a whole slope face. Depending on the site, reported rates span 0.05 to more than 5 metres per year.

Satellite InSAR revisits too slowly to catch short-term acceleration and resolves only the line-of-sight component. Ground GNSS is continuous and precise, but measures single points. UAV photogrammetry maps the entire face, yet only within a short summer window. Active ground radar and LiDAR draw more power than a year-round Arctic site can supply.

Qaarusuup Qaqqaa is included in Greenland’s inventory of unstable slopes and registered mass movements.

02 / Fieldwork in Greenland

Making the measurement possible.

The 2026 campaign ran from 10 July to 9 August, across several Greenland slopes reported as unstable. This season was a prototype trial: build the instrument, get it recording in the field, and find out whether the measurement works at all. Everything shown below comes from Qaarusuup, where the prototype was deployed from 1 to 4 August.

Once installed, the instrument stayed on site and recorded on a schedule with nobody beside it. Timing, power, storage, and signal quality decide whether there is any data to process. In this project they are as much a part of the research as the algorithms.

03 / My research

From detecting a slope to measuring how it moves.

Within the NNA Greenland Hazards Project, my work is the radar system and the signal processing behind it. The aim is not only to observe an unstable slope, but to measure quantitatively how that rock is deforming.

The system transmits nothing of its own. It reuses navigation satellites that already illuminate the slope: one antenna records the direct signal, from which the receiver reconstructs a clean reference waveform, while a second antenna points at the rock face and records the reflection. With no transmitter to power, the instrument can stay out where there is no radar infrastructure.

Many satellites are overhead at once, so the same slope is seen from many directions at the same time. This provides the geometric diversity needed to resolve displacement in three dimensions rather than along a single line of sight.

The sky-facing antenna receives the direct GNSS signal, while the slope-facing antenna receives its reflection. The USRP B210 converts these analogue electrical signals into synchronized digital IQ samples, which the single-board computer currently stores for later processing.

The planned next step is to run the processing chain on the single-board computer and send processed results back to my home institution. Target outputs include interferograms from repeat acquisitions and quantitative slope-displacement estimates, once phase stability and the displacement retrieval have been validated.

04 / From signals to images

Find the echo. Then find its place on the slope.

Raw IQ samples preserve the amplitude and phase of the received signals. Turning those recordings into a spatial image requires satellite tracking, signal compression, and a model of the observation geometry.

  1. 01

    Record & track

    Record both channels, track the satellite, and reconstruct a reference waveform.

  2. 02

    Recover the echoes

    Compress the signal in range. Inspect delay and Doppler to separate the reflected return.

  3. 03

    Focus an image

    Combine phase-preserving pulses using satellite motion and bistatic back-projection.

  4. 04

    Connect to terrain

    Use ArcticDEM elevation in image formation and examine the result against the rock face.

Range-Doppler maps summarize the echoes; SAR formation uses the underlying complex pulses, before power averaging discards their phase.

05 / Research outputs · preliminary

From a return to a terrain-referenced image.

The moving GNSS satellite provides a synthetic aperture while the ground receiver stays fixed. Here, approximately 597 seconds of phase-preserving observations are combined into a SAR image.

Why terrain belongs in the processing.

To place an echo on the map, the processor first has to be told where the ground is. Assume the ground is flat, and everything coming back from the slope piles onto a single arc consisting of flat-plane points at the corresponding distance. Give the processor the real height of each pixel from ArcticDEM instead, and those same echoes spread out across the rock face they actually came from.

Both images below are built from the same recording and drawn on the same colour scale. The only thing that changes is the terrain the processor was told to assume.

What these results establish—and what remains open

These field data support echo detection and focused imaging of the scene. Slope deformation has not yet been measured. Repeat observations, long-term phase stability, and image registration remain part of the work needed to turn this into a deformation-monitoring capability.

06 / My role

Engineering the connection between fieldwork and measurement.

I develop and integrate the radar instrument, deploy it in the field, and develop the processing chain from raw IQ data to terrain-referenced radar imagery. This is the instrument-to-image work presented here within the broader NNA Greenland Hazards Project.

07 / What next

From a prototype demonstration to a full autonomous deployment.

2026 field campaign — prototype demonstration

  • Deployed a prototype GNSS passive radar at Qaarusuup.
  • Collected raw IQ data and demonstrated range-Doppler, SAR imaging, and backscatter mapping.
  • Established the feasibility of remotely observing the rock slope.

2027 field campaign — full autonomous deployment

  • Build and deploy the complete autonomous monitoring system in July and August 2027.
  • Harden the system for a year of unattended polar operation: power through the winter, an enclosure and antennas that tolerate ice, wind and freeze-thaw, and timing stable enough to compare acquisitions months apart.
  • Leave the system in place for one year for repeated observations.
  • Target: continuous repeat-pass SAR / interferometric measurements for slope deformation monitoring.
