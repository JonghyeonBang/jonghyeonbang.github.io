01 / The problem

Steep slopes.
Consequences across the fjord.

When a large rock slope collapses into a fjord, the displaced water can generate a tsunami and threaten communities along the coast. Understanding how unstable slopes change before failure is an important part of understanding that hazard.

But these rock faces are difficult to reach. Monitoring needs to work across water, on steep terrain, and beyond a short field visit.

Qaarusuup Qaqqaa is included in Greenland’s inventory of unstable slopes and registered mass movements.

02 / Fieldwork in Greenland

Making the measurement possible.

During the July 10–August 9, 2026 campaign, I deployed GNSS passive radar equipment and collected data from slopes reported to be unstable. The results below come from the Qaarusuup deployment, August 1–4.

At Qaarusuup, the instrument remained on site after installation, recording scheduled captures without an operator beside it. Reliable timing, power, storage, and signal quality are as much a part of this research as the image-processing algorithms.

03 / My research

Use the satellites.
Build the instrument.

Within the NNA Greenland Hazards Project, my work focuses on the radar system and the signal processing needed to observe unstable rock slopes.

The system does not transmit its own radar pulse. It listens to navigation satellites: one antenna records their direct signals, while another points toward the slope to record reflections. Comparing the two provides a reference for recovering weak echoes.

04 / From signals to images

Find the echo. Then find its place on the slope.

Raw IQ samples preserve the amplitude and phase of the received signals. Turning those recordings into a spatial image requires satellite tracking, signal compression, and a model of the observation geometry.

  1. 01

    Record & track

    Record both channels, track the satellite, and reconstruct a reference waveform.

  2. 02

    Recover the echoes

    Compress the signal in range. Inspect delay and Doppler to separate the reflected return.

  3. 03

    Focus an image

    Combine phase-preserving pulses using satellite motion and bistatic back-projection.

  4. 04

    Connect to terrain

    Use ArcticDEM elevation in image formation and examine the result against the rock face.

Range–Doppler maps summarize the echoes; SAR formation uses the underlying complex pulses, before power averaging discards their phase.

The bright return sits near 1.3 km of bistatic range—the extra path travelled by the reflected signal, not the distance from receiver to slope.

This view shows that an echo is present. It does not, by itself, provide a unique map of where each reflection originated on the rock face.

05 / Research outputs · preliminary

From a return to a terrain-referenced image.

The moving GNSS satellite provides a synthetic aperture while the ground receiver stays fixed. Here, approximately 597 seconds of phase-preserving observations are combined into a SAR image.

Why terrain belongs in the processing.

A flat-ground assumption and a terrain-aware reconstruction place the same echoes differently. These outputs use the same observation and colour scale; incorporating elevation changes the geometry used to focus the image.

What these results establish—and what remains open

These field data support echo detection and focused imaging of the scene. Slope deformation has not yet been measured. Repeat observations, long-term phase stability, and image registration remain part of the work needed to turn this into a deformation-monitoring capability.

06 / My role

Engineering the connection between fieldwork and measurement.

I develop and integrate the ground-based GNSS passive radar system: antennas, software-defined radio, single-board computing, and the recording workflow. In Greenland, that work extends to installing the instrument and collecting usable observations.

I also develop the processing chain that follows the data home—from raw IQ and satellite tracking to range–Doppler analysis, SAR image formation, and terrain integration. My contribution is this instrument-to-image workflow within the broader hazards project.

07 / Research direction

Toward repeated observations of changing slopes.

The next step is to move from a field demonstration to reliable repeat measurements: improve autonomous operation, test coherence between acquisitions, and investigate whether phase differences can resolve slope deformation. The aim is a practical ground-based observation capability for places that are difficult to monitor in person.
